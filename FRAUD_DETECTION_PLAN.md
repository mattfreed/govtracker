# Fraud detection plan

Edition 2026-11-27. Generated by `scripts/build_site.py` from the project's plan file; the same plan is on the site under **Plan**. The site does not host the raw data: every source it cites links to where the record can be downloaded.

## The six questions asked of every dollar

1. **Follow the money.** Start from who was paid, by which department, under which contract, awarded how.
2. **Check the paperwork.** Was it bid? Approved before it started? Sized under a line? Amended later?
3. **Look for the relationship.** Gifts, donations, lobbying, jobs, board seats and investments linking payer and payee.
4. **Test the claim.** What did the money buy? Compare stated results with dollars, with other providers, and with how many such people exist.
5. **Check the context.** Write down the most likely honest reason. If the record that would prove it exists, ask for it.
6. **Grade the evidence.** Proven, charged, official finding, or lead. Never upgrade a lead by repeating it.

## Working definitions

- **Fraud** A deliberate, material deception to obtain money or another benefit. Screens look for conflicting representations, repeated beneficiaries and corroborating records. Intent requires separate evidence.

- **Waste** Avoidable cost or loss of service relative to a realistic alternative. Compare price, scope, timing, constraints and outcomes before estimating any loss.

- **Abuse** Use of entrusted authority or public resources for an improper purpose. Test who exercised authority, what rule or duty applied, who benefited and which exceptions were authorized.

A budget-policy difference does not establish missing cash or the denial of a particular student service. [Records](https://sfgovtracker.com/#case/city-college-reserve-policy-budget)

- **Can 24 locally delinquent contributions be traced to state reports, local periodic disclosures and a specific amended amount?** Tested. Progress San Francisco sent 24 late-contribution disclosures to the state. San Francisco voters were entitled to find them at the city filing office. The Ethics Commission imposed $29,300. The filings do not trace a particular donor’s dollars to a particular expenditure. The settlement concerns disclosure and supplies no evidence of candidate coordination or payment of the penalty. [Records](https://sfgovtracker.com/#case/progress-sf-state-and-city-contribution-reports)

- **Could agreement on flyer distribution change the legal classification of spending even when candidates did not approve the message?** Tested. The Labor & Working Families Slate reported a flyer opposing Bilal Mahmood. The Ethics Commission assigned part of its cost to Dean Preston’s campaign and assessed $2,513. [Records](https://sfgovtracker.com/#case/labor-slate-coordinated-supervisor-flyer)

- **When an intermediary provided coordinated services, which payment amounts were already included within the same assessed contribution?** Tested. Neighbors for a Better San Francisco Advocacy paid Riff City Strategies to handle media for the Chesa Boudin recall. The Ethics Commission assessed $53,916 for the missing disclosures. [Records](https://sfgovtracker.com/#case/neighbors-advocacy-recall-media-services)

- **Did the 2015 campaign audit record a return of unused public money and a separate enforcement penalty receipt?** Tested. The final audit records $27,894.30 in unexpended public funding returned to the City, $120,043 in public financing and no material audit findings. ## Nonprofits and grants

### Deals with insiders  ·  run

*Traditional scheme.* A funded nonprofit buys from, rents from or lends to a company owned by its own officer or their family.

- **What it looks like in the records:** A “yes” on the tax return’s related-party questions; Schedule L transactions; the same person on both sides of a lease.
- **Data:** IRS Form 990 Part IV and Schedule L; City payments
- **Screen:** `the “irs990 flags” analysis`
- **Found this edition:** 43 City-funded organizations report business with insiders.
- **How to trace it yourself:**
  1. Open the organization’s Form 990, Part IV lines 25–28: any “Yes” means an insider transaction.
  2. Read Schedule L for the name, relationship and amount.
  3. Compare the amount with what the City paid the organization that year.
  4. Ask for the lease, loan or contract and who approved it.
- **Still needed:** Property records for the address in each lease.

### A grant with no organization behind it  ·  run

*Traditional scheme.* Public money goes to a nonprofit with no staff, which passes nearly all of it to one contractor.

- **What it looks like in the records:** Zero or one employee; one contractor receiving most of the spending; a new organization with a large first grant.
- **Data:** IRS Form 990 Part V, VII, IX; City payments
- **Screen:** `the “irs990 flags” analysis, the “ngo master” analysis`
- **Found this edition:** Listed under “No staff, one contractor” and the “New and large” signal on organization pages.
- **How to trace it yourself:**
  1. Find organizations whose first City payment is large (the “New and large” filter).
  2. Open the 990: employees (Part V line 2a) and the five highest-paid contractors (Part VII section B).
  3. If one contractor takes most of the money, look up who owns it.
- **Still needed:** Secretary of State business filings for the contractors (blocked to automated access).

### Service numbers that do not add up  ·  run

*Novel scheme.* An organization reports more people served, or meals delivered, than is plausible — or reports the same number every year.

- **What it looks like in the records:** Identical sentences across years; a count close to the whole population it draws from; cost per person far from peers; money up while the count falls.
- **Data:** IRS Form 990 Part III and Schedule O text; Census and caseload counts; City payments
- **Screen:** `the “claims” analysis`
- **Found this edition:** Read 410 organizations; 44 repeat an identical figure three or more years running; 9 show City money up and people served down. 6 state a count larger than their neighborhood and at least a tenth of the city; 15 stated counts tripled or fell by two thirds in a year while City money stayed level. Children’s-services money per child rose from $924 to $2,595 as the number of children fell.
- **How to trace it yourself:**
  1. Open three consecutive Form 990s and compare Part III line 4 word for word.
  2. Divide program expenses by the number served; compare with similar programs.
  3. Compare the number with how many such people exist (seniors, children, people counted homeless).
  4. Ask the funding department for the units of service the organization invoiced.
- **Still needed:** Departments’ contract performance reports (units of service by year).

### One number for the City, another for the regulator  ·  run

*Novel scheme.* An organization tells its funder one story about how many people it serves and files a different count with a regulator that licenses it, or stops filing at all.

- **What it looks like in the records:** Counts filed with the State falling while government money rises; identical figures two years running; a licensed site missing from the regulator’s filings; books that do not reconcile from one tax return to the next.
- **Data:** State clinic utilization reports (HCAI); IRS Form 990 balance sheets; City payments; federal shelter inventory
- **Screen:** `the “clinics” analysis`
- **Found this edition:** 3 City-funded clinic operators report local-government money up and visits down in their own State filings; 12 licensed San Francisco clinics appear on the State’s list of clinics that did not file; 23 clinic-years show at least twice the usual visits per provider.
- **How to trace it yourself:**
  1. Find the organization’s licensed sites in the State’s annual clinic reports and note patients, visits and “county” or “local” funds for each year.
  2. Check the non-respondent sheet for years it is missing.
  3. Line up net assets at the end of one Form 990 with the start of the next, plus the surplus or loss; any gap needs a line that explains it.
  4. Compare with City payments for the same years.
- **Still needed:** The funding departments’ close-out reconciliations; licensing data for child care, residential care and meal programs (being downloaded).

### Money up, results flat  ·  run

*Novel scheme.* Spending on a problem rises year after year while the published measure of the problem does not improve.

- **What it looks like in the records:** Two lines that should move in opposite directions and do not.
- **Data:** City payments by department; federal homeless counts; overdose deaths; City scorecards
- **Screen:** `the “outcomes system” analysis`
- **Found this edition:** Homelessness payments to nonprofits up 226% since fiscal 2019; the count of homeless people changed by -1%.
- **How to trace it yourself:**
  1. Pick a department and total its payments to nonprofits by year.
  2. Find the official measure of the problem it funds.
  3. Chart each on its own axis; compute dollars per person.
  4. Ask which contracts report results, and how they are checked.
- **Still needed:** Per-contract outcome data from each department.

### Money passed along a chain  ·  run

*Traditional scheme.* A City grantee re-grants public money to other organizations, including ones that share its leaders, with no City selection process.

- **What it looks like in the records:** Schedule I grants to other City-funded nonprofits; A grants to B while B grants to A; the same director on both boards.
- **Data:** IRS Form 990 Schedule I; officer lists
- **Screen:** `the “grant chains” analysis, the “xlink money” analysis`
- **Found this edition:** 184 grants from one City-funded nonprofit to another ($145 million); 14 two-way pairs; 39 between organizations that share a leader.
- **How to trace it yourself:**
  1. Open Schedule I of the grantor’s return.
  2. Look up each recipient in the City payment file.
  3. Compare the two organizations’ officer lists.
  4. Ask which City agreement allowed the re-grant.
- **Still needed:** Sub-grant agreements held by the funding departments.

### Paid twice for the same cost  ·  run

### Funded while not in good standing  ·  run

*Traditional scheme.* An organization keeps receiving money while delinquent with the state charity registry, revoked by the IRS, or on the Controller’s corrective-action list.

### Paid-for beds standing empty  ·  run

*Novel scheme.* A provider is paid to operate housing or shelter that sits partly empty year after year.

- **What it looks like in the records:** Occupancy under 85% in two consecutive federal inventories.
- **Data:** HUD housing inventory by provider
- **Screen:** `the “housing supply” analysis, the “outcomes” analysis`
- **Found this edition:** On the dashboard updated 11/12/2026, 761 of 9,198 site-based units were vacant (8.3%, against the department's own 7% target): 90 available for referral, 308 with a move-in in progress and 363 offline. Over the twelve months from 2025-12 to 2026-11 vacancies averaged 778 a month (373 offline, 406 being filled) and never fell below 744; offline units went from 414 in 2025-12 to 363 in 2026-11 (low 329), while units being filled rose from 340 to a peak of 450. Largest offline reasons: janitorial maintenance 77, property hold 68, internal transfer 59. The excess over the 7% target is about 117 units. At the homelessness department's own pre-purchase estimate of $19,800 a unit a year for operations and services, an average of 373 offline units is about $7,385,400 a year of budgeted operating cost; at the $30,047 later estimated for one site, $11,207,531. A new building in lease-up, Harbor Haven, had 22 of 67 counted units empty (expected 125 units), outside these counts.
- **How to trace it yourself:**
  1. Find the provider’s sites in the federal housing inventory.
  2. Compare beds with people in them on the count night, two years running.
  3. Ask the department what it paid per occupied bed.
- **Still needed:** Nightly occupancy by site from the homelessness department.

### New name after trouble  ·  run

*Traditional scheme.* After a bad audit or debarment the same people continue under a new organization.

- **What it looks like in the records:** A new tax ID with the same address or officers as a recently defunded one.
- **Data:** IRS returns; business registrations; City payments
- **Screen:** `(to build)`
- **Found this edition:** 1 newer payee names are tied to a troubled one: 1 to an organization on the City’s suspended and debarred lists, 0 to a firm whose State contractor licence is shown as suspended, and 0 to a registration the Treasurer closed. The largest: KM 106 Construction Inc., paid $1,811,649 since, tied to Azul Works, Inc (5: same address only). 1 older affiliates that were already City payees more than a year before the action are left out, as is the one row already shown in the site’s “People and roles” page. 30 listed organizations and 16 payees with a suspended licence were tested; for a suspended licence the tied firm is listed only if its own licence was issued, or its City payments began, after July 2019.
- **How to trace it yourself:**
  1. List organizations whose payments stopped after an audit.
  2. Search their officers’ names in other organizations’ returns.
  3. Compare addresses in the business registry.
- **Still needed:** Secretary of State officer filings.

The lower band contains 363 contracts across 343 combinations in total. The $10 million to under $10.5 million band contains 39 contracts across 39 combinations, of which 0 have at least two contracts.

### Year-end spending rush  ·  run

*Traditional scheme.* Money is committed in the last weeks of the budget year so it is not lost.

- **What it looks like in the records:** A spike of purchase orders in June.
- **Data:** Purchase orders by date
- **Screen:** `the “forensics” analysis`
- **Found this edition:** On orders whose payments did not begin before their stated date, June carries 8% of new purchase-order value; an even spread would be 8%. For most order value the stated date comes after spending began, so it is not the order date.

### Bidders taking turns  ·  run

*Traditional scheme.* Competitors agree who wins, or a bidder is fed inside information.

  3. Look for rotation and for losers hired as subcontractors.

### Local-business fronts  ·  partly

*Traditional scheme.* A certified local firm is named to win preference points but does little of the work.

- **Screen:** `the “bid subcontract review” analysis, the “lbe fronts” analysis, the “aliases” analysis`
- **Found this edition:** On 38 contracts, a subcontractor carrying the local-business flag works under a prime contractor or joint-venture member that the directory records as its affiliate (38 pairings, 8 distinct firm pairs, $6.4 million in listed subcontract shares). The most frequent: LAVINA TRUCKING, INC. under ESQUIVEL GRADING & PAVING INC, on 22 contracts ($5.1 million). 70 more pairings of recorded affiliates on one team are of other kinds (two subcontractors, or the certified firm as the prime).

- **What it looks like in the records:** A grant reported as one line; claims reduced after review; City payments to vendors above what the federal program approved.

- **Screen:** `the “federal view” analysis`
- **Found this edition:** FEMA approved $312 million of the City’s pandemic claims and later took back $4 million. The City’s $625 million recovery grant is reported to Treasury as a single line of payroll. On the federal measure, 17.4% of people leaving shelter went to permanent housing, against a peer median of 35.8%. Median cost of a tax-credit apartment: $880,503.
- **How to trace it yourself:**
  1. Pull every FEMA project for the applicant and total approved, obligated and de-obligated amounts.
  2. Compare a program’s FEMA-approved cost with City payments to its vendors.
  3. Read the recovery-fund project list for what the grant is said to have bought.
  4. Set the City’s results beside peer cities on the federal measures.
- **Still needed:** FEMA determination memos and appeal decisions (the site blocks automated readers); the City’s claim packages.

## Health and benefit billing

### Federal money used where only State money may be used  ·  run

*Traditional scheme.* A state covers residents whom federal law excludes, but bills Washington for part of the cost anyway, through an outdated formula, unreturned rebates or miscoded claims.

- **What it looks like in the records:** An inspector-general finding of improper claiming; repeated federal deferrals on the same item; State budget lines for repayments.
- **Data:** Federal and State statutes; HHS inspector general audits; federal Medicaid agency letters; Legislative Analyst reports; State enrolment totals by county. No person-level or location data.
- **Screen:** `the “eligibility” analysis`
- **Found this edition:** The federal inspector general found California improperly claimed $53 million of federal Medicaid money in nine months. The federal agency deferred a further $16 million on status-related items in May 2026, and has withheld $120 million over unreturned drug rebates. The State’s own cost for this coverage is $10.80 billion a year; 23,061 San Francisco residents are enrolled.
- **How to trace it yourself:**
  1. Read the statute that authorizes the benefit and note whose money it names.
  2. Find the inspector general’s audits of how the State splits federal and State costs.
  3. Read each quarterly federal deferral letter for repeat items.
  4. Check the State budget for repayments to the federal government.
- **Still needed:** The State health department’s cost estimate and its replies to the federal agency (its website blocks automated readers).

### Benefits paid in error or by fraud  ·  run

*Traditional scheme.* Food aid, cash aid or in-home care paid to people who do not qualify, paid in the wrong amount, or billed for care not given.

- **What it looks like in the records:** An error rate above the federal 6% line; fraud findings falling while the caseload rises; almost nobody disqualified; computer matches left pending.
- **Data:** State reports by county: fraud investigation activity (DSS 466), overpayments (CA 812), income matches (DPA 482), CalFresh error rates and caseload; District Attorney arrest file. Who receives benefits is confidential and is not examined.
- **Screen:** `the “benefits” analysis`
- **Found this edition:** Food aid issued in San Francisco rose from $79 million to $247 million a year. About 12% is paid in error by the federal measure, roughly $30 million a year; fraud identified by investigators was $466,321. Between 3 and 30 people were disqualified in the latest year. San Francisco ranks 15 of 26 large counties for investigations concluded per household.
- **How to trace it yourself:**
  1. Take benefits issued and the error rate for the year; multiply for an order of magnitude.
  2. Count investigations concluded, with and without evidence, per 10,000 households, and compare with other large counties.
  3. Count people disqualified by hearing or in court.
  4. Ask the county how many computer-match discrepancies were left unworked and why.
- **Still needed:** In-home care paid hours by county; overissuance claims by county for food aid; general assistance fraud-unit figures (requested from the State’s published files; several are not published).

## Nonprofits and grants

### Audit findings that repeat  ·  run

*Traditional scheme.* An organization’s own auditors report the same weakness year after year, question costs or doubt it can survive, and the public money keeps coming.

- **What it looks like in the records:** Repeat findings, material weaknesses, questioned costs or a going-concern paragraph in the federal single audit.
- **Data:** Federal Audit Clearinghouse; City payments
- **Screen:** `the “record” analysis`
- **Found this edition:** Federal audits are on file for 161 City-paid organizations (816 audit reports, 2019 to 2025). Auditors reported findings at 43 of them, and at 7 in three or more separate years; those 7 were paid $351 million by the City since FY2019. 22 of the 122 findings were marked as repeats of an earlier one, 30 were graded material weaknesses and 16 involved questioned costs. 3 parent organizations that file one audit covering several City payees (hospital systems among them) are listed last and left out of these counts.
- **How to trace it yourself:**
  1. Look the organization up in the Federal Audit Clearinghouse.
  2. Read each finding’s type and whether it repeats a prior year.
  3. Read the corrective-action plan and check the next year’s audit for the same finding.
  4. Compare with what the City paid in those years.
- **Still needed:** The City’s own monitoring letters for the same years.

### Paid for more than the licence or the payroll can hold  ·  run

*Novel scheme.* Public money per licensed place, bed or employee far above every comparable provider, or money rising while the licence and the head count shrink.

- **What it looks like in the records:** Money per licensed place several times the median; City money up while payroll and benefit-plan head counts fall; a contract listed after the licence closed.
- **Data:** State licensing files; Form 5500; IRS Form 990; State child-care contracts; City payments
- **Screen:** `the “capacity” analysis`
- **Found this edition:** Of 410 City-paid nonprofits with payroll counts on their tax returns (276 also file a staff benefit-plan return), 13 saw City payments rise by a quarter or more while their payroll count fell by a tenth or more; the largest is Catholic Charities, where City payments went from $20.7 million to $33.4 million as the payroll count went from 602 to 537. 10 organizations paid $1 million or more a year have fewer than one person on payroll for every $250,000 of City money, led by Reality House West, Inc with 1 on payroll and $3.6 million from the City; the middle organization of that size receives $42,240 per person.
- **How to trace it yourself:**
  1. Find the licence: capacity, status and closing date.
  2. Divide the public money by the licensed places.
  3. Compare the head count on the tax return with the benefit-plan filing.
  4. Ask how many sites and how much pass-through money explain the gap.
- **Still needed:** Per-site attendance records, which only the funding department holds.

## Health and benefit billing

### Paid far above the national norm  ·  run

*Novel scheme.* A provider is paid many times what every other biller in the country receives for the same service.

- **What it looks like in the records:** Total above the national top 1% for a service; pay per claim line several times the national and California figures; a new provider number billing millions within a year.

- **Screen:** `the “medicaid” analysis`

### Budget add-backs  ·  run

*Traditional scheme.* Each June the Board adds money to the Mayor’s budget. Some lines name an organization or a supervisor’s own district, outside any grant competition.

- **What it looks like in the records:** A line naming one organization; the same purpose added back year after year; district-tagged money; a recipient with other warning signs or political giving.

- **Screen:** `the “earmarks” analysis`

## Contracts and purchasing

### Small contract, grown by amendment  ·  run

*Traditional scheme.* A contract starts small, sometimes under an approval line, and is then amended to several times its size without a new competition.

- **What it looks like in the records:** A not-to-exceed amount that doubles or more across amendments; a start just under $10 million; an amendment approved after its start date.
- **Data:** Board of Supervisors files
- **Screen:** `the “record” analysis`
- **Found this edition:** 159 agreements at least doubled and grew by $10 million or more through amendments the Board approved from 2014 to 2026; 23 grew five times or more. 80 of the 159 began between $9 million and $10 million, just under the amount at which the Board must approve a contract, and now stand at $2.97 billion together. 63 are with profiled nonprofits and 141 are with payees that carry other warning signs on this site.
- **How to trace it yourself:**
  1. List every Board file for the agreement.
  2. Compare the first amount with the latest.
  3. Check whether each amendment was competed or justified as sole source.
- **Still needed:** Amendments below the Board threshold, which never reach a file.

### A company formed for the contract  ·  run

*Traditional scheme.* A business is registered days or weeks before it wins City work, or opens at the address of a barred contractor.

- **What it looks like in the records:** Registration within 90 days of the first contract; a non-competitive award; the same address or owner as a suspended or debarred firm.
- **Data:** Registered businesses; supplier contracts; debarment lists
- **Screen:** `the “people” analysis`
- **Found this edition:** 27 San Francisco-registered payees with at least $250,000 in City payments give a business start date no more than 90 days before their first City contract; 10 of those first contracts are not recorded as competitively bid and those payees have been paid $88.8 million since.
- **How to trace it yourself:**
  1. Compare the registration date with the first contract date.
  2. Check how the contract was awarded.
  3. Look up the owner and address against the debarment lists.
- **Still needed:** State business filings naming the officers (blocked to automated access).

### Barred here, paid there  ·  run

*Novel scheme.* A firm the City has suspended or flagged keeps winning work from the school district, City College, BART or another public body that keeps separate books.

- **What it looks like in the records:** A debarred or suspended name in another body’s board approvals; approvals after the fact; sole-source or emergency awards.
- **Data:** Board documents of seven public bodies; City debarment lists
- **Screen:** `the “earmarks” analysis`
- **Found this edition:** 2 firms on the City’s suspended-and-debarred list appear in 9 board items of other bodies worth $96.1 million as read; 2 items ($4.3 million) are dated after the City acted. The largest is ProVen Management, Inc.: 8 BART items, $75.6 million, of which 2 ($4.3 million) are change orders and settlements dated after the City suspended it.
- **How to trace it yourself:**
  1. Search the other body’s board approvals for the name.
  2. Compare dates with the City’s suspension.
  3. Check how the award was made.
- **Still needed:** Payment-level ledgers for these bodies, which are not published.

## Businesses, property and permits

### Buildings bought high, resold fast or rented from insiders  ·  run

*Traditional scheme.* The public buys or leases a building at a price well above comparable deals, soon after it changed hands, or from someone connected to the buyer.

- **What it looks like in the records:** Price per home far above the City’s other purchases; a sale shortly before the public purchase; repair estimates that grow after closing; a nonprofit renting from its own officer.
- **Data:** Board of Supervisors files; assessor rolls; tax returns
- **Screen:** `the “property” analysis`
- **Found this edition:** The Board approved 41 purchases of property inside the city since 2014, $1,045,663,961 in all (including one $326,690,953 deal to have a new City office building built); 9 of them, $370,000,000, were buildings for homeless housing. Where the number of homes is known (9 buildings: 7 of the homeless-housing purchases and 2 other residential buildings) the price runs from $200,000 a home (333-7th Street) to $852,941 (333-12th Street), with a middle value of $268,919. The two official counts of homes at 333-12th Street differ: the State’s Homekey award list gives 170 ($852,941 a home) and the Board’s Budget and Legislative Analyst, in the report on the purchase file and in the 2024 audit, gives 200 ($725,000 a home). The records do not say why the counts differ.
- **How to trace it yourself:**
  1. Find the Board file: price, seller, units.
  2. Compare price per home with the City’s other purchases.
  3. Check the assessor roll for the last sale date.
  4. Read the appraisal attached to the file.
- **Still needed:** The appraisals and the recorder’s sale prices.

### Permits that need permits  ·  run

*Novel scheme.* One job needs many separate permits from several departments, and a permit that expires while waiting must be bought again to close the first.

- **What it looks like in the records:** A permit whose only purpose is to finish, renew or reinstate an earlier one; a street permit that quotes a building permit; a violation notice for an expired permit.
- **Data:** Building, plumbing, electrical, street and planning permit records
- **Screen:** `the “permit process” analysis`
- **Found this edition:** Since 2015, 8,860 building permits (2.6% of all filed) were taken out only to get a final inspection on, renew or reinstate an earlier permit; the earlier permit was a median 4.4 years old, and 44% were five or more years old. 541 lots needed three or more such permits. Of full-review permits issued for filings from 2012 to 2019, 6% were later named by a finishing or renewal permit and 23% still show no final sign-off.
- **How to trace it yourself:**
  1. Look up every permit at the address.
  2. Read each description for “to complete work under” or “renew”.
  3. Count departments and days from first filing to final sign-off.
- **Still needed:** The published rulebook and fee schedules, to confirm which steps are required by law.

### The paid shortcut through the queue  ·  run

*Traditional scheme.* Applicants who hire a registered permit consultant move differently through review, and some consultants’ plans keep landing with the same plan checker.

- **What it looks like in the records:** Faster issue than like permits; one reviewer handling an outsized share of one consultant’s permits; fees reported to the Ethics Commission.
- **Data:** Permit contacts and routing; Ethics Commission consultant reports
- **Screen:** `the “permit process” analysis, the “permits” analysis`
- **Found this edition:** 12,196 building permits filed 2015 to 2024 list a permit consultant or expediter (4.3% of all; 30% of full-review jobs of $5 million and over, 9.7% of those under $50,000). Set against like permits of the same kind, cost band and year, the answer is mixed: on jobs sent for full plan review the consultant's permit came out ahead (229 days against 289), but across all permits it was faster only 44% of the time (alterations approved at the counter: median 14 days against 15 for like permits without one; alterations sent for full plan review: median 229 days against 289 for like permits without one; new buildings: median 303 days against 306 for like permits without one). In their own filings, 46 consultants and firms reported $6.7 million in client fees.
- **How to trace it yourself:**
  1. Compare the permit with others of the same kind, cost and year.
  2. List who reviewed it at each station.
  3. Check the consultant’s quarterly reports for the client and the officials contacted.
- **Still needed:** Inspection sign-off records naming the inspector, which the City does not publish.

### Who gets a yes  ·  run

*Novel scheme.* Like proposals end differently depending on the district, the neighborhood, and who files them.

- **What it looks like in the records:** A district or neighborhood whose refusal-and-withdrawal rate sits beyond chance after adjusting for the kind of proposal; review requests concentrated in a few areas; different outcomes for consultants’ and donors’ projects.
- **Data:** Planning Department records; Census neighborhood profiles; campaign, lobbying and consultant registers
- **Screen:** `the “decisions” analysis, the “planning records” analysis`
- **Found this edition:** Among 28 neighborhoods with at least 50 decided applications, the adjusted share not approved runs from 21.7% (West of Twin Peaks) to 42.5% (Outer Mission); 1 is beyond what chance would explain: Outer Mission (31 of 59 not approved against 19 expected). Neighbors’ review requests run from 9 per 1,000 full residential permits in Western Addition to 115 in Marina (city 46).
- **How to trace it yourself:**
  1. Pick one kind of proposal and one period.
  2. Compare the share not approved across districts with the counts beside each rate.
  3. Read the hearing minutes for the cases behind an outlier.
- **Still needed:** Roll-call votes, appeal decisions and commission minutes (being collected).

## The City’s own books

### Spending far above comparable governments  ·  run

*Novel scheme.* A government spends several times what comparable governments spend per resident on the same function, year after year.

- **What it looks like in the records:** Spending per resident by function against peers; rank that never moves; liabilities per resident.
- **Data:** State Controller financial reports for cities and counties
- **Screen:** `the “fiscal” analysis`
- **Found this edition:** In fiscal 2025 San Francisco spent $10,517 per resident on general public services. The middle of 7 other large California cities was $2,582, which makes San Francisco look 4.07 times as costly; adding each city’s county, the middle of 6 city-plus-county pairs was $5,855, and San Francisco is 1.8 times that (rank 1 of 7). Against the pairs it is furthest above the middle on debt payments (2.94 times) and health, welfare and sanitation (2.56 times), and lowest on buildings and equipment (0.91 times). It collected $7,062 in taxes per resident against a pair middle of $2,456.
- **How to trace it yourself:**
  1. Take the State Controller’s report for each government.
  2. Add the city and its county, because San Francisco is both.
  3. Divide by residents and compare function by function.
- **Still needed:** A common chart of accounts: the function grouping is ours.

### What the City’s own auditors found  ·  run

*Traditional scheme.* The outside auditors of the City’s accounts and federal grants report weaknesses, questioned costs or findings that repeat.

- **What it looks like in the records:** A qualified opinion; a material weakness; the same finding in consecutive years.
- **Data:** Annual Comprehensive Financial Reports and Single Audits, fiscal 2015–2025
- **Screen:** `the “fiscal” analysis, the “record” analysis`
- **Found this edition:** 11 findings in 11 years. 2 were rated a material weakness: 2016-002 (Special Tests and Provision: Maximum Per-Unit Subsidy and Underwriting Requirements); 2020-002 (Period of Performance). The heading that recurs most is “Procurement and Suspension and Debarment”: findings in fiscal 2015 and 2016, after one under the same heading the year before (2014-004), about different programmes (Port Security Grant Program; Assistance to Firefighters Grant). The following year’s report said corrective action had been taken on 11 of the 11 findings it followed up. 1 finding was a repeat (2015-001, repeating a fiscal 2014 finding the report said had not been fixed). 3 findings give dollar figures under the “questioned costs” heading; the rest say there were none or leave it blank.
- **How to trace it yourself:**
  1. Open the Single Audit for the year.
  2. Read the summary of auditor’s results and the schedule of findings.
  3. Check the next year’s report for whether each finding was resolved.
- **Still needed:** Single Audits for fiscal 2021 to 2025 are in the documents library but not yet read into this screen.

## Businesses, property and permits

### What must be bought before a permit  ·  run

*Novel scheme.* The rules require an applicant to pay third parties, hold insurance, give notice or obtain another permit before the City will accept or issue the one applied for.

- **What it looks like in the records:** A stated prerequisite; a requirement to hire a licensed professional; a fee that multiplied; two permits that each need the other.
- **Data:** The City’s published permit pages, fee tables and ordinances 2018–2026; State housing reports
- **Screen:** `the “permit rules” analysis`
- **Found this edition:** 57 published requirements were found: 18 to use a licensed architect, engineer or contractor, 19 to hold insurance or post a bond, 4 for a meeting before applying, 8 for a notice to neighbors, and 7 for a separate street or sidewalk permit. 36 of them send money to a private professional, insurer or surety, whose prices the City does not set or print. ## Contracts and purchasing

### Emergencies and waivers that never end  ·  run

*Traditional scheme.* Purchasing rules allow exceptions for emergencies, sole sources and exempted services. The exception becomes the routine, and the same suppliers collect one after another.

- **What it looks like in the records:** Emergency authority still in use years after the event; a department buying mostly without competition; a supplier with several kinds of exception; a sole-source award to a new firm.
- **Data:** Purchasing rule recorded on each payment and contract; Board files; departments’ waiver lists
- **Screen:** `the “exceptions” analysis`
- **Found this edition:** From fiscal 2019 to fiscal 2026 the City paid $4.08 billion under rules that set competition aside: $1.49 billion under emergency powers, $1.52 billion as sole source, $761.8 million where an ordinance waives bidding and $303.0 million under other "not bid" rules. That is 7.5% of the $54.52 billion of payments that record any rule; 51% of all payment dollars record none. The total peaked at $703.4 million in fiscal 2021 and was $695.4 million in fiscal 2026, when payments under ordinance waivers ($394.5 million) had replaced most of the emergency spending.
- **How to trace it yourself:**
  1. Read the purchasing rule recorded on the contract.
  2. Check the date against the emergency or waiver it cites.
  3. Ask for the written justification.
  4. Look for the same supplier under other exceptions.
- **Still needed:** The justification memos, which are not published.

## Nonprofits and grants

### Money in, expected result out  ·  run

*Novel scheme.* Money is paid year after year for a result nobody outside the provider records, or against a target that is missed and then made easier.

- **What it looks like in the records:** No published result by provider; a contract objective with no reported figure; a target eased after a miss; a measure dropped after falling short; more money after a failed review.
- **Data:** City performance measures and targets; monitors’ reports; contract objectives; federal and State counts; independent evaluations
- **Screen:** `the “expected” analysis`
- **Found this edition:** Of $2.09 billion paid to nonprofits in fiscal year 2026, $1.37 billion (66%) went to providers for which no provider-level result recorded by anyone else is on disk. Published provider results exist only in Public Health (40% of its payments go to a provider with at least one published review) and Homelessness (78%). 24 departments that paid nonprofits $91.5 million have no City target for those services, no outside count and no provider result at all, led by City Administrator ($29.1 million), Arts Commission ($15.6 million), Sheriff ($12.5 million), Recreation and Parks ($6.9 million).
- **How to trace it yourself:**
  1. Find the objective in the contract appendix.
  2. Look for the result in the department’s monitoring report, not the provider’s own.
  3. Check whether the target changed the year after a miss.
  4. Divide the money by the independent count.
- **Still needed:** Provider-level results held by the funding departments and not published.

### Two sets of books  ·  run

*Novel scheme.* The City’s ledger and the organization’s own tax return describe the same money and do not agree, or the return does not add up against itself.

- **What it looks like in the records:** City payments above reported revenue; net assets that do not roll forward; opening figures that differ from last year’s closing figures; one contractor taking most of the spending; a paid year with no return.
- **Data:** City payment ledger; IRS Form 990 returns 2020–2025
- **Screen:** `the “reconcile” analysis, the “xlink intake2” analysis`
- **Found this edition:** Across 2,001 organization-years in which the City paid 463 organizations $100,000 or more, 12 organizations had at least one year in which City payments alone exceeded the total revenue on the organization's own tax return, by $28.6 million in their worst years combined; the largest is San Francisco Public Health Foundation, paid $13.5 million in the tax year ended 30 June 2022 against reported revenue of $1.6 million. For 7 of them the City's payments exceed reported revenue over every year on file added together, for 2 the gap is covered by a fall in money owed to them on their own balance sheet, and for 3 the return offers no clue to the reason. A further 9 organizations reported enough revenue but spent less in total than the City alone paid. 21 other one-year gaps disappeared when the year was pooled with its neighbour and are not listed.
- **How to trace it yourself:**
  1. Add up City payments for the organization’s tax year.
  2. Compare with government grants and total revenue on the return.
  3. Roll net assets forward from the opening balance.
  4. Ask for the audited statements where they do not meet.
- **Still needed:** Audited financial statements, which most organizations file only with the State charity registry.

## Contracts and purchasing

### The same winners, year after year  ·  run

*Novel scheme.* The same payees collect a department’s money every year, by one exception after another, with no new competition and little turnover.

- **What it looks like in the records:** A top-ten list that barely changes; a payee with an exception in every year; a contract number alive for eight years; a payee that vanishes and a like-sized newcomer the next year.
- **Data:** City payments and contracts fiscal 2019–2026; Board files; campaign and lobbying records
- **Screen:** `the “winners” analysis`
- **Found this edition:** 52 payees drew three or more different kinds of exception spread over four or more of the eight fiscal years; 17 drew at least one in every year. Together they were paid $6.25 billion, $1.73 billion of it under rules that set competition aside; 45 are nonprofits and 47 carry warning signs elsewhere. At the top: Tenderloin Housing Clinic Inc (5 kinds over 8 years); Healthright 360 (5 kinds over 8 years); Community Housing Partnership (5 kinds over 8 years); Felton Institute (5 kinds over 7 years); Homeless Prenatal Program (5 kinds over 7 years). The exceptions screens count how many kinds stack on each supplier; this one shows the order they came in.
- **How to trace it yourself:**
  1. List a department’s ten largest payees each year.
  2. Count how many names repeat and how each was awarded.
  3. For a repeat payee, list the exception used each year.
  4. Ask for the last competitive solicitation.
- **Still needed:** Solicitation files and scoring sheets, which are not published.

## Businesses, property and permits

### Bought or leased soon after it changed hands  ·  run

*Traditional scheme.* A building is sold, and within months the public leases or buys it from the new owner at a higher figure.

- **What it looks like in the records:** A sale date shortly before the City’s lease or purchase; a reassessed value far below the City’s price; a building sold again soon after a new City lease.
- **Data:** Assessor rolls 2007–2025 for every building in the City’s property deals; Board files; State acquisition-grant lists
- **Screen:** `the “parcels” analysis`
- **Found this edition:** The prior sale date falls within one year of the public purchase in 2 cases, within two in 5 and within three in 8; 3 of those are finished buildings comparable like for like: 681-687 Ellis Street (295 days, +39%); 1000 Sutter Street (Hotel Granada) (477 days, +53%); 888 Post Street (727 days, +190%). 3 more fall between three and four years. 12 matched purchases show no earlier sale date on any roll from 2007 to 2025; for 3 of them the land value was nonetheless re-set in some year, which means a change of ownership or reassessment the roll does not date.
- **How to trace it yourself:**
  1. Look up every roll year for the parcel.
  2. Find each change in the recorded sale date and the value set after it.
  3. Set the City’s price and date beside the last one.
  4. Pull the deed and transfer-tax declaration for the price itself.
- **Still needed:** Recorded deeds and transfer-tax declarations: the roll shows a sale’s date and its trace in the assessed value, not the price.

## The City’s own books

### A public body whose own overseers keep failing it  ·  run

*Traditional scheme.* A district spends more each year on fewer pupils while its State overseers rate it at high risk, its auditor cannot give an opinion, and the same failings repeat from one review to the next.

- **What it looks like in the records:** A high-risk rating repeated; a disclaimed audit; central-office cost far above like districts; contracts approved after work began; a system bought, repaired and replaced.
- **Data:** Federal school-finance survey; State results files; State fiscal-crisis team reviews; auditors’ and accreditor’s reports; board agendas
- **Screen:** `the “schools” analysis, the “schools records” analysis`
- **Found this edition:** The State team answered "no" (a failing) to 54 questions in 2022, 64 in 2024 and 60 in 2026. 16 questions were answered "no" in all three reviews, among them: audit findings left uncorrected; staffing records not used to build the budget; actual revenue and spending out of line with the budget; deficiencies named in State oversight letters not addressed; no board-approved plan to control transfers from the general fund; falling enrolment; school buildings not fully used. 10 of the 20 sections had more failings in 2026 than in 2022; between 2024 and 2026 the count rose in budget development and adoption, charter schools, deficit spending (unrestricted general fund), general fund - current year, information systems and data management, leadership and stability, special education.
- **How to trace it yourself:**
  1. Read the State fiscal-crisis team’s latest review and the one before.
  2. List the questions answered “no” in both.
  3. Set spending per pupil by function against like districts.
  4. Read the auditor’s opinion page for each year.
- **Still needed:** The district’s own audits, budget books and interim reports, which sit on hosts that bar automated collection.

## Nonprofits and grants

### Child-care money passed on with no names  ·  run

*Novel scheme.* Most early-childhood money goes to a few agencies that pay it on to providers and families; the public record names almost none of the final recipients, and no record counts the children paid for at each site.

- **What it looks like in the records:** Onward payments reported as one unnamed total; contracts worth more children than the licence holds; money listed for a centre whose licence has closed; dollars per child rising as children fall.
- **Data:** City payments and vouchers; State contracts and licensing files; meal-program records; the agencies’ tax returns
- **Screen:** `the “daycare” analysis, the “capacity” analysis`
- **Found this edition:** In fiscal 2025 the City paid Children's Council of San Francisco $113.5 million (or $149.2 million, counting $35.7 million that the City's file labels fiscal 2025 although its voucher numbers and dates fall in June 2024) and the State promised it up to $35.9 million; its tax return shows $209.7 million of government money received, $186.8 million paid onward (89% of spending), $17.6 million in pay and benefits and $6.3 million in other costs, so the return balances to a surplus of $2.1 million. Onward payments rose from $96.8 million in 2021. The grants schedule names no onward recipient: the whole sum is reported as grants to individuals in 4 lines ("Child care Subsidies and Child care Provider Stipends": 2,547 recipients, $100.6 million; "Quality Grants": 119 recipients, $31.9 million; "ECE Workforce Compensation Enhancements": 2,551 recipients, $52.4 million; "Nutrition": 222 recipients, $1.9 million; ECE on the return stands for early care and education). The same is true of Wu Yee Children's Services ($49.4 million paid onward in 2025). The only providers either return names are its five highest-paid contractors (next screen); the Low Income Investment Fund names its recipients (the screen after). The City's own nonprofit-spending dataset shows $4.4 million to Children's Council for the same year, because it leaves out the two largest agreements. Its City agreements on file list 172 objectives, and no published result against them was found.
- **How to trace it yourself:**
  1. Find the agency’s onward payments on its tax return.
  2. Ask the funding department for payments by provider.
  3. Set each provider’s funded children against its licensed places.
  4. Check the licence status on the payment dates.
- **Still needed:** Funded slots and enrolment per site, and site-level meal counts: none is published.

## Payroll and benefits

### A job made for a person  ·  run

*Traditional scheme.* A position is created, retitled or kept outside the examined service so that a chosen person can hold it, or a permanent job goes to the person already sitting in it.

- **What it looks like in the records:** A class with one holder; a dormant class revived for one newcomer; a permanent appointment to someone already in the seat, alone on the list; pay above the published scale; generic titles multiplying.
- **Data:** City payroll with employment type; civil-service appointments; published pay scales; disclosure forms
- **Screen:** `the “jobs” analysis`
- **Found this edition:** Of 22,243 permanent civil-service appointments since October 2018 that can be matched to the payroll, 3,290 (14.8%) went to someone already paid in that class in that department the fiscal year before on another footing: 1,922 as temporary exempt, 930 as permanent exempt and 438 as provisional. 325 of those 3,290 were the only person ever appointed from their eligible list, and 683 were ranked first on it. Among departments with 50 or more appointments the share is highest in Technology (50.3%), Mayor (40.8%) and Homelessness Services (36.9%). 9,005 appointments (40.5%) went to people not on the payroll the year before.
- **How to trace it yourself:**
  1. Find the appointment in the civil-service file.
  2. Look for the same person in that class and department the year before.
  3. Check how many people were on the eligible list.
  4. Ask for the job announcement and its dates.
- **Still needed:** Job announcements with posting and closing dates, and exam records: not published.

### Private grants the return cannot hold  ·  run

*Novel scheme.* Foundations’ own returns show money paid to an organization that its return does not show as received, or the same people sit on the funder and the recipient.

- **What it looks like in the records:** Grants paid exceeding contributions reported; private money appearing only on the government line; a director of the funder who is an officer of the recipient; money routed through an intermediary.
- **Data:** Returns of 3,700 Bay Area and large California foundations 2020–2025; recipients’ returns; Form 700 filers; behested payments
- **Screen:** `the “foundations” analysis`
- **Found this edition:** Of 1,751 organization-years where both sides are on file, 2 show grants from these funders alone larger than every contribution and grant the recipient reported in that year and the years either side: San Francisco Public Health Foundation 2021 ($2.3 million paid by 4 funders, $203,030 reported, total revenue $1.5 million); African Advocacy Network 2021 ($154,000 paid by 3 funders, $112,461 reported, total revenue $800,916). A further 27 organization-years (19 organizations, $30.6 million) fit inside total contributions only if the private grants were entered on the return's government-grants line; the largest gaps are Heluna Health 2024 ($3.6 million in private grants, $371,286 reported outside the government line); Young Community Developers Inc 2021 ($2.7 million in private grants, $42,887 reported outside the government line); Main Street Launch 2022 ($2.5 million in private grants, $0 reported outside the government line).

## Payroll and benefits

### Salaries, extra pay and bonuses  ·  run

*Novel scheme.* Pay far above the scale or the organization’s size, bonuses paid in a bad year, and extra pay that dwarfs salary.

### Groups, not single organizations  ·  run

*Novel scheme.* A nonprofit, its contractor, a consultant, an official and a shared address act together; each looks ordinary alone.

- **What it looks like in the records:** Shared officers and addresses; money passing between members; several kinds of warning sign inside one group; a firm paid by many funded nonprofits.
- **Data:** Every analysis file; tax-return officer, contractor and grant tables; business registrations
- **Screen:** `the “rings” analysis`
- **Found this edition:** 112 linked groups were found among 2,305 flagged organizations, firms and addresses. The top-ranked group is named after Felton Institute: 6 members carrying 46 different kinds of warning sign and $426.9 million of City money since fiscal 2019; of its members none is a party to a proven or charged case, one is the subject of an official finding of its own, and 3 are named in the file of a proven or charged case without being a party; attached to it by one route: Gwendolyn Westbrook, party to a charged case (charged; not decided, 2026-02-23). Across all 112 groups, 4 hold a member that is itself a party to a proven or charged case, 18 hold a member that is the subject of an official finding, and 4 hold a member that is only named in such a case file.
- **How to trace it yourself:**
  1. Look for: Shared officers and addresses; money passing between members; several kinds of warning sign inside one group; a firm paid by many funded nonprofits.
  2. In these records: Every analysis file; tax-return officer, contractor and grant tables; business registrations.
- **Still needed:** State business filings naming owners and officers.

### Order in time, and who resembles the known cases  ·  run

*Novel scheme.* A gift or donation, then a decision, then an amendment or a job: the order tells more than any one event. And the proven cases left a pattern others may share.

- **What it looks like in the records:** Three or more kinds of event between the same payee and official inside two years; donations bunched before decisions more than chance allows; the same signs the proven cases showed before they broke.
- **Data:** Campaign, gift, lobbying and requested-payment records; Board files; contracts; case timelines
- **Screen:** `the “chains” analysis`
- **Found this edition:** Counting only the official who decided or sponsored a decision, a gift or donation from the payee’s side falls in the 90 days before it 21 times; with the decision dates shuffled 2,000 times the average is 21.9 and 57% of the shuffles show as many or more: no more than chance would produce. Counting every supervisor who sat when the Board approved it, the real count is 116 against a shuffled average of 102.3 (16% of the shuffles show as many or more): no more than chance would produce. Both are null results. Lobbying contacts, which should cluster before decisions if the method can see timing at all, number 123 against a shuffled average of 84.7 (within what the shuffles produce).
- **How to trace it yourself:**
  1. Look for: Three or more kinds of event between the same payee and official inside two years; donations bunched before decisions more than chance allows; the same signs the proven cases showed before they broke.
  2. In these records: Campaign, gift, lobbying and requested-payment records; Board files; contracts; case timelines.
- **Still needed:** Exact payment dates, which the City’s payment tables do not carry.

## Officials: gifts, influence and politics

### What lobbyists are paid, what they pay out, and what their clients get  ·  run

*Novel scheme.* Money flows from clients to lobbyists and from lobbyists’ circles to officials; decisions follow for the clients.

- **What it looks like in the records:** Client payments beside decisions won; giving by a lobbyist’s family, staff or firm after the lobbyist ban; fundraising arranged for officials lobbied; payments at an official’s request by a lobbyist’s client.
- **Data:** Lobbyist registrations, contacts, payments and activity reports; campaign records; Board files; permits
- **Screen:** `the “lobbyists” analysis`
- **Found this edition:** 136 passed Board files about a contract, lease, agreement or other money matter name as counterparty a client that was paying a lobbyist around that time; 49 of them name a sponsor (the rest came from departments with none). In 13 the client’s lobbyists had reported contacting the sponsor in the three years before passage, in 8 the client’s circle had given to the sponsor’s committees, and in 2 both. All four links (fees paid, contact with the sponsor, giving to the sponsor, sponsorship of a file with a dollar amount) are present in 1 sponsor-and-client pair covering 1 file, with $1,000 of giving beside $10.0 million stated in the file. Supervisors the circle had given to were sponsors in 23.8% of 21 cases against 8.8% of 411 for other sitting supervisors (a gap beyond chance: 5 against 1.5 expected when the giving is shuffled among the supervisors sitting for each file). A final Board roll call is on disk for 126 of the 136 files (7 of them read from the Clerk’s minutes; 10 of the others passed before 2015): 117 passed with no vote against and 9 were divided. In the rows below, a sponsor who was on that roll call voted aye in 17 of 17 cases, no in 0, and was excused or absent in 0; in 4 more the sponsor was not on the roll call (4 of them files whose sponsor of record is “Mayor”). 38 more files are gifts or grants a lobbying client made to the City; they are listed but not counted. 2 of the 17 budget add-backs that name an organization name a lobbying client.
- **How to trace it yourself:**
  1. Look for: Client payments beside decisions won; giving by a lobbyist’s family, staff or firm after the lobbyist ban; fundraising arranged for officials lobbied; payments at an official’s request by a lobbyist’s client.
  2. In these records: Lobbyist registrations, contacts, payments and activity reports; campaign records; Board files; permits.
- **Still needed:** Fee agreements, which are not filed: a success fee cannot be seen in public records.

### Approvals nobody votes against  ·  run

*Novel scheme.* Contracts, leases, grants and budgets reach the Board already negotiated; if almost none is ever opposed, the vote is not where anything is checked.

- **What it looks like in the records:** Share of money files passed without a single no; files sent on without a recommendation or hurried to the next meeting; long lists passed on one identical vote; members voting on a file that names a declared source of income.
- **Data:** Board and committee roll calls on money files and lobbied measures, 2015 on; the Clerk’s minutes; Board rules; Form 700 statements
- **Screen:** `the “boardvotes” analysis`
- **Found this edition:** From 2015 to 9 November 2026 the Board cast a recorded final vote on 3,483 contracts, leases, grants, settlements and appropriations. 3,359 (96.4%) passed without a single no vote, 2,703 of them with every listed member voting aye. 121 passed over at least one no and 3 were voted down. By year the share passed with no dissent runs from 92.6% (2016) to 99.7% (2019), with no steady rise or fall. The 124 files with a no vote were decided on 82 separate vote lists: 47 of them are one member’s no repeated down 9 same-day lists of 3 or more files.
- **How to trace it yourself:**
  1. Look for: Share of money files passed without a single no; files sent on without a recommendation or hurried to the next meeting; long lists passed on one identical vote; members voting on a file that names a declared source of income.
  2. In these records: Board and committee roll calls on money files and lobbied measures, 2015 on; the Clerk’s minutes; Board rules; Form 700 statements.
- **Still needed:** Why a member was printed “Excused”, and the consent-agenda headings of each meeting, which the vote pages do not carry.

### Drawings where the same names keep winning  ·  run

*Novel scheme.* Something scarce is handed out by lottery, waiting list or rotation, and the same applicants, firms or addresses win far more often than their share of entries allows.

- **What it looks like in the records:** Repeat winners beyond chance; winners that share an address, owner or agent; a pool where one member takes most of the task orders; entries filed in blocks.
- **Data:** Published drawing results: condominium conversions, liquor and cannabis licences, taxi medallions, vending and street spaces, contractor pools, grant rounds, housing lotteries (aggregate)
- **Screen:** `the “lotteries” analysis`
- **Found this edition:** Only one drawing on disk publishes entrants and winners for more than one year: the condominium-conversion lottery of 2001 to 2013. There the same buildings did not keep winning: 804 of 1,996 buildings won once, 7 are marked winners twice and 1,185 never won. What stands out is the reverse: a first entry won 3.3% of the time and a building on its 8th entry 71.6%. The longer a building had been entering, the better its chance; that is what the code prescribes, since Subdivision Code section 1396.3 gives a building one extra ticket for every lottery it lost, with no cap. Even with tickets weighted that way, about 85 first entries would have been expected to win and 54 did, and about 118 sixth-or-later entries against 237: the remaining gap is in the direction expected from the separate Pool A, which converts buildings that lost three or more times ahead of the ticket draw. The State’s 2026 neighborhood liquor-licence drawing had 8 entrants for 5 licences and is the only year online, so no entrant can be seen winning twice. The taxi medallion list was a queue, not a draw.
- **How to trace it yourself:**
  1. Look for: Repeat winners beyond chance; winners that share an address, owner or agent; a pool where one member takes most of the task orders; entries filed in blocks.
  2. In these records: Published drawing results: condominium conversions, liquor and cannabis licences, taxi medallions, vending and street spaces, contractor pools, grant rounds, housing lotteries (aggregate).
- **Still needed:** Full entrant lists, which most drawings do not publish.

### Commissioners whose own firms do business with the City  ·  run

*Novel scheme.* An appointed commissioner’s company holds City contracts, permits or leases, sometimes before the commissioner’s own body.

### Agency time, applicant time and open applications  ·  run

*Traditional scheme.* Calculate comparable durations for issued permits and age for still-open permits using the published snapshot.

- **What it looks like in the records:** Long review after a complete submission, repeated visits to a station, or a delay beyond a rule applicable on the review date.
- **Data:** Permit status, dated routing steps, approval and issuance registers and the agency’s published performance rules.
- **Screen:** `the “permit duration” analysis`
- **Found this edition:** The published register covers 1,152,647 distinct permits. Individual records distinguish filing, approval, issuance, open age and terminal status.
- **How to trace it yourself:**
  1. Keep the original permit number.
  2. Exclude invalid or missing dates.
  3. Compare like permit types and account for applicant review.
- **Still needed:** Timestamped hold reasons, responses and reassignment history.

## Nonprofits and grants

### Sampled costs, estimated exposure and what was recovered  ·  run

*Traditional scheme.* Compare the exact audited sample, projection, final grantor decision and later payment or offset.

- **What it looks like in the records:** A projected amount is described as a realized loss, or a later audit closes the control finding without documenting recovery.
- **Data:** Federal single audits, expenditure schedules, corrective action plans, grant decisions and payment ledgers.
- **Screen:** `the “local federal findings” analysis`
- **Found this edition:** The Housing Authority’s FY2023 audit distinguished a $179,377 sample from a $44,624,677 projection. The next two inspection samples identified no questioned costs.
- **How to trace it yourself:**
  1. Match the audit year and reference.
  2. Inspect sample and population definitions.
  3. Follow later findings and final grantor determinations.
- **Still needed:** Final disallowances, repayment receipts and approved substitutions.
